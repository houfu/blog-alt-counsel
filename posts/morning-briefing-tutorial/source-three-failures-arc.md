## 3. THE THREE-FAILURES SKILL ARC

**Structure (consistent v1→v4):** vague brief → precise-but-blind brief → silent-assumptions brief; each failure teaches something about the author's own unexamined judgment; the winning version is the *shortest*.

- **Failure 1 — vagueness.** v4: "The first brief was vague: review the morning's updates and flag what is material. It flagged everything — technically defensible, entirely useless." (v1's longer-beat version: "It did this. It flagged everything.")
- **Failure 2 — the attachment.** v4: "The second specified practice areas, instrument types, timeframes. Better — until it missed a consultation paper with a two-week deadline, because the deadline sat in an attachment. The brief had said nothing about attachments. The junior had no reason to look." (v1: "My instructions had said nothing about attachments. The system had no reason to look.")
- **Failure 3 — silence read as immateriality.** v4 (the best legal-flavoured version): "The third failure was subtler: the brief covered the common cases and quietly assumed the uncommon ones would resolve themselves. They went unmentioned, and the silence was read as immateriality — a construction any drafter will recognise, usually from the losing end." (v1/v2: "They simply went unmentioned, which the system interpreted as unimportant.")
- **The punch line between failures.** All drafts: **"I rewrote each time."** (standalone one-line paragraph in v1 and v4 — keep it that way).
- **The twist — shorter won.** v3: "The third version worked, and here is the twist: it was *shorter* than the second." v4: "The brief that finally worked was also the shortest… Lawyers are trained to draft for every contingency; a junior does better with a clear checklist than a code of conduct."
- **What it all taught.** The mirror paragraph (v1→v4, nearly stable): "Every wrong output was a mirror held up to something I had not examined: an assumption about how information is structured, what counts as urgent, where a deadline appears. The system was not getting smarter. My instructions were getting clearer. The failures were not obstacles to the process. They were the process."
- **The deeper lesson.** v2: "in twenty years of practice, nothing had ever required me to describe my own professional judgment with this degree of precision — and the exercise turned out to be independently valuable… The contraption taught me that. The contraption, notably, still gets things wrong." v4: "the exercise turned out to be worth more than the tool."
- **Prehistory to graft on (dossier §4):** the 2024 3-page prompt, disqualified, becoming a 45-line skill in 2025 — "Not 'write a better prompt each time' but 'encode your judgment once, run it forever.'" Plus: "If you can write a checklist in markdown format, you can build a skill." / "Prompt engineering teaches you to write better instructions within someone else's system. Skills let you program the system itself." / SkillsBench: "models can't write their own good skills — human domain judgment required"; law is a weak-priors domain where skills add the most.
